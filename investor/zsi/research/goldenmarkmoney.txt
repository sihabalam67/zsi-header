CHOOSE THE RIGHT HANDOFF

Print or email?
Make it work for you.

Two delivery choices for a familiar document. Here’s what changes, and what your recipient needs to know.

Printed checks and emailed eChecks: delivery and recipient requirements
At a glance	Printed check	Emailed eCheck
How it reaches the payee	You hand over or send the printed check	Check document delivered by email
What you need	Compatible printer and suitable paper	Correct recipient email and check details
What your payee does	Deposits using a bank-supported method	Accesses and prints, then follows their bank’s deposit rules
Useful for	Local handoffs and paper-based workflows	Recipients you cannot hand a check to
Bank requirements	Paper, ink or toner, endorsement, and deposit policy apply	Paper, ink or toner, endorsement, and deposit policy apply

Neither method guarantees bank acceptance or immediate funds availability. An emailed eCheck is a check document; it is not an ACH transfer.

Check printing, explained.

New to printing your own checks? These are the terms you’ll see most.

MICR line
The row of numbers along the bottom of a check, printed in the E-13B font so bank scanners can read the routing number, account number, and check number.
Routing number
The nine-digit number that identifies your bank. It's the first group of numbers in the MICR line.
eCheck
A check delivered by email instead of by mail. The payee accesses and prints it, then follows their bank’s deposit requirements.
Check stock
Blank security paper made for printing checks, usually with the check at the top, middle, or bottom of the page.
Remittance stub
The detachable part of a check that lists which invoices or items the payment covers, so the payee can match it to their records.
MICR toner
Printer toner with magnetic particles. It helps bank equipment read the MICR line reliably, and some banks prefer it.
